01 — Efficiency

Waste Heat to Power

Every thermal process discards heat. Most of it is too low-grade for a conventional steam turbine and is simply rejected to atmosphere. The Organic Rankine Cycle recovers it.

An ORC uses a working fluid with a boiling point well below water’s, sealed in a closed loop. Waste heat evaporates the fluid into pressurized gas, the gas drives a turbine, and the fluid is condensed and recirculated. This closes the loop on the rest of the stack: reactor and turbine reject heat, data center cooling loops and geothermal return flows all become generation.

02 — Nuclear

Small Modular Reactors

A small modular reactor moves nuclear construction from the field into the factory. Rather than a single bespoke unit built on site over a decade, reactors are fabricated in controlled conditions, shipped and installed — compressing schedule, reducing capital risk and improving quality control.

Modularity is the commercial advantage. Capacity is added in increments as demand materializes, so a campus can start modest and scale without redesign. Modern designs rely on passive safety systems requiring no operator action to shut down safely. Beyond electricity, the same units supply high-grade process heat for desalination and hydrogen production.

Factory built, not site-fabricatedCapacity added in incrementsPassive inherent safety

03 — Hydrogen

Green Hydrogen

Electrolysis splits water into hydrogen and oxygen using electricity. When that electricity is carbon-free, the hydrogen is too — a storable, transportable fuel for industry, heavy transport and long-duration power.

We favor solid oxide electrolysis. Operating at high temperature, solid oxide cells achieve significantly higher electrical efficiency than low-temperature alternatives, particularly when integrated with waste heat. That makes them a natural pairing with the reactors, geothermal loops and heat-recovery systems elsewhere in the stack.

04 — Geothermal

Advanced Closed-Loop Geothermal

Conventional geothermal only works where hot, permeable, water-bearing rock sits near the surface — a rare geology that has kept the resource confined to a handful of regions. Closed-loop systems remove that constraint by sealing the working fluid inside a buried pipe network that behaves like a deep radiator.

Heat transfers by conduction rather than by circulating groundwater, so no aquifer is required and no fracturing is involved — removing induced-seismicity risk and the water sourcing burden that constrains conventional projects. Because hot rock exists almost everywhere at depth, siting becomes an engineering decision rather than a geological accident.

Anywhere, no special geologyZero fracking or aquifer needPassive thermosiphon circulation

05 — Intelligence

AI-Powered Smart Grids

A microgrid running reactors, geothermal, solar, hydrogen and multiple storage chemistries is a control problem before it is an engineering one. The orchestration layer forecasts load and generation, dispatches every asset against price and carbon signals, and rebalances continuously.

AI workloads make this harder than a conventional industrial load. Training clusters produce rapid, large swings in demand that can stretch the capability of on-site generation — a challenge the IEA has specifically flagged. Predictive control smooths those transients, detects faults before they cascade, and islands the campus within a cycle when conditions require it.

06 — Hybrid Architecture

Grid-Scale Storage Paired with SMRs

Nuclear and batteries solve opposite problems. A reactor delivers firm, carbon-free baseload but responds slowly and runs most economically at steady output. A battery responds in milliseconds but empties. Paired, they cover the whole envelope.

The SMR fleet carries base load at constant, optimal output while grid-scale storage absorbs the transients — training-run ramps, cooling-plant steps, momentary faults. Storage also lets the reactors run flat through low-demand hours, charging rather than throttling, which materially improves capacity factor and therefore levelized cost.

Firm 24/7 nuclear baseloadMillisecond storage responseHigher reactor capacity factor

Grid energy storage projected to grow from $18.3B in 2025 to about $260B by 2035 at 30.4% CAGR.

Baseload plus transient absorption
07 — Multi-Day Storage

Iron-Air Batteries

Lithium-ion economics collapse beyond a few hours. Iron-air chemistry changes the duration curve entirely, delivering multi-day storage from three of the most abundant materials on earth: iron, water and air.

The operating principle is reversible rusting. Discharging, the cell breathes in oxygen and converts iron to rust. Charging, current converts the rust back to iron and the cell breathes oxygen out. The electrolyte is water-based and non-flammable — no thermal runaway pathway and no heavy metals, which changes both the siting envelope and the insurance profile.

Multi-day discharge durationAbundant iron, water and airNo thermal runaway pathway

Utility planning studies point to tens of gigawatts of multi-day storage demand in the US over the next decade.

Reversible rusting — breathes O₂
08 — Storage Frontier

Solid-State Batteries

Replacing a liquid electrolyte with a solid one raises energy density, improves safety and removes the flammability that governs how conventional cells must be packaged. The obstacle has never been the chemistry — it is manufacturing at scale.

Solid-state cells demand precise, uniform densification. Conventional uniaxial pressing struggles to achieve even compaction across a large cell. Isostatic pressing applies pressure equally in every direction, and the tooling now exists to make it industrial: warm isostatic presses reaching 600 MPa at up to 145°C with automated batch throughput.

600 MPa warm isostatic pressure145°C process temperatureUniform omnidirectional compaction

Near-term commercial opportunity sits in process equipment and qualification, ahead of cell volume.

Isostatic — equal pressure, all axes
